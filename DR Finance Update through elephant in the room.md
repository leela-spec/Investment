## Regime read: what is _actually_ going on now

My synthesis is: **the world is not in a clean recession, not in a clean boom, and not in a clean disinflation regime.** It is in a **late-cycle, liquidity-tight, policy-uncertain environment** where headline growth is still being propped up by tech/AI capex and fiscal support, while underneath that surface the system is becoming more fragile. The Fed kept rates at **3.50%–3.75%** on **18 March 2026**, the ECB kept its deposit facility at **2.00%** on **5 February 2026**, and the U.S. Treasury curve on **20 March 2026** was still positively sloped with **2-year 3.90%** and **10-year 4.39%**. That is **not** a panic recession signal; it is a signal that financing conditions remain restrictive enough that **term-premium / debt-cycle risk still matters**. ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260318a.htm "Federal Reserve Board - Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement"))

The live macro backdrop is still resilient on the surface. The IMF’s **January 2026 WEO update** projects **3.3% global growth in 2026** and says technology investment, policy support, and financial conditions are offsetting trade headwinds. But the same IMF update explicitly flags downside risks from **revaluation of technology expectations** and geopolitics, and USTR opened new **Section 301 investigations on 11 March 2026**, which means tariff/deglobalization risk is active, not hypothetical. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/01/19/world-economic-outlook-update-january-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com "World Economic Outlook Update, January 2026: Global Economy: Steady amid Divergent Forces"))

The debt-cycle risk is structural, not theatrical. CBO’s long-term outlook has **debt held by the public reaching 107% of GDP in 2029**, above the post-WWII peak, with net interest continuing to rise as a share of GDP. That matters because it raises the odds that long bonds cease to function as a reliable hedge in inflationary or term-premium shocks. ([cbo.gov](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-03/61187-LTBO-Executive-Summary.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Long-Term Budget"))

Your attached Howell article is tactically clear: **global liquidity has rolled over, policy-rate expectations have risen, term premia have fallen, and the joint signal from bonds + liquidity is risk-off**; his concrete recommendation is to **de-risk, cut credit, trim U.S. stocks, reduce tech/financials, build cash, prefer short-to-mid duration, utilities, staples, gold, and commodities/resources**. Your USD-rotation transcript argues the longer-run move is **not “Trump-only,” but a broader valuation/capital-rotation process away from U.S. concentration toward Europe/Asia, helped by dollar weakness**. And your AI memo is best read as a **left-tail mechanism**: AI can initially boost margins and equities, then later break wage income, discretionary demand, private credit, and eventually prime housing assumptions if labor displacement becomes broad enough.

## Asset-class development: precise category view

### 1) Cash / bills / short-duration high grade

This is the **highest-confidence asset bucket now**. It gives carry, dry powder, and protection against being early in long-duration or risk assets. In **E** and **C**, it materially outperforms most risky assets on a risk-adjusted basis. In **A**, it lags, but the opportunity cost is acceptable because it preserves optionality. This is the first thing I would increase in an overextended portfolio. Howell’s “build cash, short-to-mid duration” is directionally right.

**Development path:**

- **0–3 months:** steady-to-strong relative outperformance
    
- **3–12 months:** still attractive unless the Fed is forced into a clear easing cycle and growth is re-accelerating
    
- **What invalidates it:** genuine broadening growth + clear disinflation + falling front-end real yields
    

### 2) Safe-haven sovereign debt: short/intermediate vs long duration

You should treat this as **two separate trades**:

- **Front end / intermediate sovereigns:** broadly attractive now
    
- **Long duration:** **not a strategic core; only a conditional macro hedge**
    

Long duration works in **B** and partially **A**. It fails in **C** and can fail badly in **E** if the move is driven by fiscal supply, term-premium repricing, or inflation persistence rather than a classic recession. The playbook you extracted is aligned with this: bonds stabilize portfolios, but drawdown and regime governors should override blind conviction.

**Development path:**

- **Short/intermediate duration:** likely good in **E/D/C**; best all-weather rates bucket now
    
- **Long duration:** high payoff only if growth shock dominates; otherwise vulnerable
    

**FX dimension:** conceptually, analyze safe-haven sovereigns as **rates exposure + currency exposure**.  
**ASSUMPTION:** Switzerland / Singapore are more interesting as **currency-quality + sovereign-quality combinations** than as pure duration trades; Norway is less a pure haven because energy exposure changes the payoff profile.

### 3) Inflation-linked bonds

These are a **cleaner stagflation hedge than nominal long bonds**. Best in **C** and **C+E**. Less useful in **B**, where outright disinflation/deflation dominates and nominal duration usually beats.

**Development path:**

- **0–3 months:** good hedge against tariff/supply re-acceleration
    
- **3–12 months:** keep if inflation remains sticky and long-end nominal bonds stay unstable
    

### 4) Gold vs industrial metals vs broad commodities

**Gold** is the best single cross-scenario hedge in your universe. It works in **D**, **E**, and **C**; it is only mediocre in **A**. In a true liquidity accident it can get sold briefly, but medium-term it benefits from fiscal dominance, reserve diversification, geopolitical stress, and skepticism about paper hedges. Howell’s article is right that gold looks underpinned.

**Industrial metals** are _not_ the same trade as gold. They need either **A** or **D** plus Chinese/global investment stabilization. They are weak in **B** and mixed in **E**.

**Broad commodities** are strongest in **C**. They can also work in **D** if global capex and ex-U.S. rotation dominate. They are bad in **B**.

**My hierarchy:**

1. **Gold** = core hedge
    
2. **Resources / energy** = cyclical inflation hedge
    
3. **Industrial metals** = conditional growth-capex expression
    

### 5) Equities: broad market and regional/style split

This is where nuance matters most.

#### U.S. equities overall

I would **trim U.S. concentration**, not because the U.S. collapses, but because the **next dollar of upside is less compelling** once you adjust for valuation, crowding, and FX for non-dollar investors. The transcript is clear that valuation gaps and political uncertainty are already encouraging capital rotation, and that this can persist structurally.

#### U.S. tech

I would **not** follow Howell literally into “dump tech” as a blanket category. I would split tech into three buckets:

1. **AI compute / infrastructure / mission-critical enablers**  
    Best in **A**, acceptable in **D**, vulnerable in **E** if long yields/discount rates stay high
    
2. **Application software / workflow / intermediation / “friction monetizers”**  
    This is the most vulnerable bucket under the AI memo: pricing compression, build-vs-buy substitution, and agentic disintermediation hit margins and multiples.
    
3. **Mega-cap duration proxies held purely for index momentum**  
    Vulnerable in **E** and **C** because the market stops paying the same multiple for long-duration earnings streams
    

#### Europe / Japan / Asia ex-U.S.

This is the clearest relative opportunity under **D**. The transcript’s logic is strong: a weaker dollar, valuation differentials, policy diversification outside the U.S., and better relative upside in Asia. Europe can work, but **Asia is the cleaner expression** of the rotation thesis.

#### Emerging markets

EM is mostly an **Asia trade** in practice. It works when:

- dollar weakens
    
- external financing pressure eases
    
- trade shock does not intensify into full global slowdown
    

It struggles if the dollar **spikes first** in a risk event. That is a critical failure mode.

#### Style buckets

- **Quality / min-vol:** best core equity style now
    
- **Value / real-asset tilt:** strong in **C** and **D**
    
- **Defensives / utilities / staples / infrastructure:** strong now; Howell is directionally right here
    
- **Financials:** weak in **B** and **E**; underweight
    
- **Cyclicals:** only overweight if **A** clearly takes over
    

### 6) Credit: IG vs HY

This is one of the cleanest calls.

- **Investment-grade credit:** only attractive in **front-end, high-quality, liquid form**
    
- **High yield:** structurally vulnerable
    
- **Private-credit proxies / illiquid yield vehicles:** highest downside asymmetry if **B** or **E** materializes
    

Howell’s call to **cut credit** is one of the parts I agree with most strongly. The AI memo reinforces that if labor displacement hits spending and private marks are slow to adjust, credit repricing can be much worse than equity investors expect.

**Development path:**

- **0–3 months:** spreads likely grind wider or stay fragile
    
- **3–12 months:** only re-risk into credit after either a genuine growth reacceleration or a full spread washout
    

### 7) VIX-futures-based volatility products

Useful, but **only tactically**. These are not holdings; they are **overlays**. The reason is simple: roll and carry bleed are real, so time works against you outside stress windows.

**Use case:**

- activate when liquidity tightens, credit spreads widen, trend structure breaks, and cross-asset vol starts to spill over
    
- reduce once the event is priced and curve shape normalizes
    

**Best scenarios:** **B, C, E**  
**Worst scenario:** **A**, or long sideways grind where you bleed without payoff

### 8) Crypto

Treat crypto as:

1. **liquidity beta first**
    
2. **alternative monetary narrative second**
    

Howell is explicit that crypto tracks liquidity closely and expects more volatility and downside when liquidity tightens.

My precise read:

- In **Howell-style risk-off / tightening liquidity**, crypto is usually **down first**
    
- In **D** and **E**, **BTC** can recover earlier than the rest because the “alternative reserve / monetary skepticism” narrative bites harder
    
- **ETH/SOL / broad alt complex** are much more dependent on risk appetite, app activity, and speculative breadth
    

So: crypto is **not** a core hedge. It is a **small, regime-dependent satellite**.
