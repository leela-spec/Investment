## Bond yields: decomposition & policy-rate expectations (as shown)
**Put differently, bond yields are rising, but the components behind the rise may be more important than the scale of the move.** In short, *terminal policy rate expectations* embedded in term structures **more than explain these jumps**, whereas *term premia* (the compensation for holding interest rate risk over the term of the bond) are falling. This tells us that bond investors see a potential **growth shock** (falling term premia) that is reinforced by rising policy rates.

Evidence the track of daily rate expectations calculated from each national term structure in the next chart. With the possible exception of already high US and UK expectations, there has been a rising trend in policy rates elsewhere. And, in the past 2-3 weeks, interest rate expectations everywhere have jumped higher.

## Falling term premia & “safe asset” demand (as shown)
This largely explains higher bond yields, even though it coincides with falling bond term premia. **Falling term premia is significant news: term premia are positively affected by:** (1) **rising inflation uncertainty**; (2) **higher volatility** and (3) **excess supply of ‘safe’ assets.** The consensus would claim all three should be pushing up term premia. Yet, Japan aside, World bond term premia had largely flat-lined to late-February and are now skidding lower. Taken together, **this likely evidences a growing demand for ‘safe asset’ government debt.** See below.

## “Rates up, term premia down” summary & yield-curve signal (as shown)
Let’s collect this evidence together and summarise it in the following chart. The chart highlights the response of aggregate World term premia and policy rate expectations. The divergence (rates up, term premia down) is important because it suggests that **bond investors expect policy makers to hike rates and potentially push economies towards recession.** The combination of rising rate expectations and falling term premia translates into a **flattening yield curve** (the US 10-2 spread has narrowed by some **25bp** since early February) – itself often a ‘Risk Off’ signal – and is consistent with a **tightening liquidity backdrop.** In contrast, a rising inflation outlook would surely be signalled by a steepening curve?
