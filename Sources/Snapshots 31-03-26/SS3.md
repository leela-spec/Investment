- **Bonds**: Global bond markets show a nuanced picture: yields are still relatively high versus the 2010s, with term premia and fiscal concerns important drivers; credit has been more resilient than equities.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w4DmliR8Bvg)

## 2. Historical patterns: wars, currencies, and bonds

Broadly, three channels matter: inflation/oil, growth/recession risk, and government borrowing/fiscal stress.

**Bonds and yields**

- In big 20th‑century “hot wars” (World Wars, Korea, Vietnam), bond yields often rose because economies were already running hot and wars added inflation and large deficit financing (“guns and butter” in Vietnam).
    
- In the post‑1980 disinflationary era, conflicts like the Gulf War and post‑9/11 often coincided with or accelerated easing cycles: after the first Gulf War, 10y US yields ultimately fell ~80 bps; after 9/11 they fell about 100 bps over the following year, as recession and Fed cuts dominated.
    
- A more recent example of conflict plus high inflation is Russia–Ukraine: yields moved from ~2% to about 5% between 2022 and late 2023, but that reflected an inflation and policy regime shift that the war amplified, not the war alone.[](https://realinvestmentadvice.com/resources/blog/treasury-yields-dont-lie-but-wars-dont-drive-them/)
    

**Currencies**

- The US dollar has repeatedly acted as a **safe‑haven**; capital tends to flow into Treasuries and USD assets at the onset of shocks, even when yields are rising.
    
- During Middle East and oil‑shock episodes (1973–74 embargo, 1990 Kuwait, early 2000s Iraq), currencies of major oil importers have tended to weaken against exporters and the USD, especially when oil spikes feed inflation and trade‑balance deterioration.
    
- EM FX and high‑yielding carry currencies historically sell off when geopolitical risk spikes and global liquidity tightens.
    

**Equities and credit**

- War onsets often trigger an initial equity drawdown and volatility spike, but returns during the full war period have been mixed and heavily dependent on the starting macro/valuation backdrop.
    
- Credit spreads usually widen around the shock and then stabilize or retrace if the conflict is contained and growth/inflation remain manageable.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w4DmliR8Bvg)
    

## 3. Middle East escalation specifics (including US ground forces)

For a scenario where the current US–Iran theater escalates and the US deploys ground forces, the most important **macro transmission channel is oil and shipping**.

Key historical analogues and patterns:

- **Oil price shocks from Mideast wars**
    
    - 1973–74 embargo: coordinated supply cuts led crude to roughly quadruple from about 3.5 to over 11 USD per barrel, helping to trigger recession and stagflation.
        
    - 1990 Kuwait invasion: Brent jumped sharply as markets priced potential large disruptions, though the spike was relatively short once the military path was clear.
        
    - 2003 Iraq War: onset saw about a 46% increase in oil prices, tied to supply fears and geopolitical risk.
        
- **Recent Israel–Iran episodes and Red Sea disruptions**
    
    - In 2023–24, tensions around Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran, plus Houthi attacks in the Red Sea, pushed oil up near or above 80 USD, but moves were often faded as markets reassessed actual supply loss versus fear.
        
    - In 2025’s Israel–Iran war, Brent rose roughly 15% and then largely retraced as physical flows through the Strait of Hormuz remained intact, highlighting how much the built‑in geopolitical premium has fallen.[](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/israel-iran-war-highlights-mideasts-declining-influence-oil-prices-2025-06-25/)
        

If the US uses ground forces and the conflict truly broadens (higher perceived risk to Gulf production and shipping), historical precedent suggests:

- A **larger and more persistent oil spike** than recent episodes, but likely less than the 1970s “step‑change” unless exports are structurally blocked (e.g., Strait of Hormuz obstruction).
    
- Higher global headline inflation and downside risk to growth, particularly for big importers (euro area, Japan, India, parts of EM Asia).
    
- Tighter financial conditions through either higher real yields (if inflation worries dominate) or wider credit spreads and lower equities (if growth fears dominate).[](https://id.allianzgi.com/en-id/insights/outlook-and-commentary/fixed-income-forward-march-2026)[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w4DmliR8Bvg)
    

## 4. What this typically means for FX

Assuming a material escalation with US ground forces and higher oil:

1. **US dollar (USD)**
    
    - Likely to **appreciate** near term as the global safe‑haven, especially versus EM FX and risk currencies, even if US fiscal costs rise.
        
    - If the conflict drives a global slowdown and pushes other central banks into more dovish stances relative to the Fed, rate differentials can support a stronger USD for longer.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n-E0r-S5nRk)
        
2. **Euro (EUR), yen (JPY), pound (GBP)**
    
    - EUR and GBP are exposed as net energy importers; persistent higher oil often weakens them versus USD, especially if local growth is hurt and the ECB/BoE lean more dovish.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n-E0r-S5nRk)
        
    - JPY’s traditional safe‑haven role is diluted by BoJ’s still‑low rate structure; in recent episodes, USD/JPY has sometimes risen (yen weakening) when US yields rose on inflation/fiscal concerns despite risk aversion.
        
    - If the escalation produced a full‑blown risk‑off and global yields fell, JPY could regain some haven bid versus higher‑yielding currencies, but the starting point is much trickier than in past decades.[](https://realinvestmentadvice.com/resources/blog/treasury-yields-dont-lie-but-wars-dont-drive-them/)[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Xw_JmMplmg)
        
3. **EM FX and commodity currencies**
    
    - EM importers of oil (e.g., many in Asia) tend to see FX pressure from terms‑of‑trade deterioration and outflows during risk‑off episodes.
        
    - Commodity exporters (parts of LatAm, some African and Middle Eastern currencies) can benefit from better trade balances, but this is often overshadowed by generalized risk aversion and weaker global growth expectations.
        
4. **Safe‑haven and funding currencies**
    
    - CHF and, to a lesser degree, JPY may still attract flows as “insurance” assets in tail‑risk scenarios, but the USD typically dominates the move.
        

## 5. What this typically means for bonds

The sign and size of bond moves depend on whether **inflation risk or growth/recession risk** dominates, given the starting point of elevated inflation and big fiscal deficits.

1. **US Treasuries**
    
    - Textbook “war = lower yields” often fails when inflation and deficits are already high. In the recent US–Iran conflict (air and naval), 10‑year yields actually rose from about 3.96% to roughly 4.26% in the first week of fighting, even as money flowed into USD, because markets focused on inflation and fiscal reality.[](https://realinvestmentadvice.com/resources/blog/treasury-yields-dont-lie-but-wars-dont-drive-them/)
        
    - A ground‑force escalation with a meaningful oil shock could push **nominal yields higher** initially (inflation and issuance worries), especially at the long end, even if real yields are more stable.
        
    - Over a longer horizon, if oil‑driven inflation forces central banks to stay tighter and growth slows, you can get a sequence of: higher yields and steeper curves at the onset, then eventual bull‑flattening if recession risk materializes and the market starts to price future cuts.
        
2. **Other developed‑market bonds**
    
    - Euro area and UK curves are particularly sensitive to imported energy inflation plus weaker growth; markets might price **stagflation risk**, with breakevens up and real yields not falling as much as in prior cycles.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n-E0r-S5nRk)
        
    - JGBs are constrained by the BoJ’s framework, but sustained higher imported inflation from energy can push the BoJ to stay tighter than otherwise, limiting their traditional role as a pure safe asset.
        
3. **Credit markets**
    
    - Investment‑grade spreads might widen modestly, with resilient balance sheets and still‑decent fundamentals helping absorb the shock unless the conflict triggers a major global downturn.[](https://id.allianzgi.com/en-id/insights/outlook-and-commentary/fixed-income-forward-march-2026)[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w4DmliR8Bvg)
        
    - High yield and leveraged credit are more exposed to a growth‑shock scenario; spreads could widen more sharply if higher energy and weaker demand compress margins for non‑energy sectors.[](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w4DmliR8Bvg)[](https://id.allianzgi.com/en-id/insights/outlook-and-commentary/fixed-income-forward-march-2026)
        
4. **Sovereign risk and EM debt**
    
    - Wars and explicit war threats historically show up in higher sovereign spreads, particularly for countries close to or directly involved in conflict.[](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/EMBI_and_Wars_post_008730c3-1126-4374-bafa-05610505cd9d.pdf)
        
    - EM local‑currency debt has recently performed well helped by weaker USD and disinflation; a strong USD plus higher commodity import bills would challenge that, while commodity exporters with credible policy might still offer some resilience.
