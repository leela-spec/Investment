The historical pattern is not “war = buy everything after the first shot.” The more robust mechanism is: **(1)** at the onset, markets price **oil, shipping, and safe-haven demand**; **(2)** if the conflict looks short and shipping is secured, oil often peaks and risk assets rebound; **(3)** if the conflict looks prolonged, the market shifts from “uncertainty discount” to **inflation, deficits, and growth damage**. The Iraq-war literature found war probability lifted spot oil by roughly **$10/barrel** and cut U.S. equities by about **15%**, with damage concentrated in discretionary/travel/IT and benefits to energy and gold; separately, EIA says Hormuz carries about **20% of global petroleum liquids** and about **20% of global LNG trade**, which makes this setup more systemically inflationary than a simple Iraq replay. ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9587?utm_source=chatgpt.com "What Do Financial Markets Think of War in Iraq? | NBER"))

This matters more now because the U.S. enters the shock with already-heavy borrowing needs and an unfavorable debt backdrop: Treasury estimated **$574 billion** in privately held net marketable borrowing for **Jan–Mar 2026**, and CBO’s long-term outlook says debt held by the public reaches **107% of GDP in 2029**. Your uploaded Howell note also explicitly argues that global liquidity is rolling over, that bonds are flashing **risk-off**, and that the preferred mix is **cash, short-to-mid duration, reduced credit, gold, utilities/staples, and less U.S. beta**; your capital-rotation transcript argues that any later USD weakness should be read as **structural capital rotation away from U.S. concentration**, not as a pure Trump-only story. ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0377?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Treasury Announces Marketable Borrowing Estimates | U.S. Department of the Treasury"))

Your own uploaded war notes already pointed to the key asymmetry I agree with: **higher oil starting point, USD up first, and bonds no longer a clean hedge**.

## My current asset-class read

**Base path:** this would most likely begin as **stagflationary risk-off**, not classic deflationary panic.

- **Oil / energy:** up first almost regardless; only peaks hard if Hormuz normalization becomes believable.
    
- **USD:** up first on safety and global funding stress; later vulnerable if the war becomes fiscally sticky and U.S.-specific.
    
- **Gold:** medium-term bullish, but short-term can be messy if real rates jump or funds liquidate winners.
    
- **Treasuries:** **front-end safer than long-end**; long duration is not a clean first-order hedge because oil, issuance, and fiscal concerns can push yields higher before recession fear eventually drags them lower.
    
- **Credit:** IG front-end survives better; HY and illiquid credit are the obvious losers.
    
- **Equities:** defense, energy, utilities, staples outperform; airlines, discretionary, small caps, housing, and levered cyclicals underperform.
    
- **Crypto:** usually down first as liquidity beta; only later can BTC re-rate if the narrative turns toward fiscal stress / monetary debasement.

## Where this differs from 1991 and 2003

This is the part many investors get wrong.

- **1991 / 2003 logic:** once the war begins, uncertainty falls, oil can peak, equities can rally.
    
- **2026 wrinkle:** because Hormuz is systemically larger than Iraq alone, because oil already starts high, and because U.S. debt issuance is already heavy, a ground move can **raise yields and inflation expectations at the same time that it hurts growth**. That is worse for classic 60/40 behavior.
    

That is also why I do **not** like the lazy trade of “war = buy long Treasuries.”  
This time the cleaner sequence is:

**cash / front-end quality / gold / inflation linkers / defense-energy-defensives**  
before  
**long duration / broad beta / lower-quality credit**.
